Back to the residency plan

The camera is a tool for engagement before it is a tool for documentation. Take the shot, then show the kid. A camper who feels seen has a better day, and a parent who sees that has a better summer. Everything on this page works toward both.

This page is both things at once: the hub we work from this weekend, and the guide you keep after. It fills in as we go. Each day's debrief lands here, your cameras and your light get written into it, and by Sunday it is Kingswood's own field guide, not a generic one. The link never changes.

It is built to sit alongside the training this team already runs, not on top of it. Where your own program covers something, this page defers to it and adds what a visiting photographer can add: low light, the hours nobody usually shoots, and the habits that keep a whole summer of pictures findable.

Quick reference

  • Faces first. Get close, then closer. Shoot at their eye level.
  • Wipe the lens. Focus on the eye. Light on faces, never behind them.
  • A variety of perspectives: wide and tight, high and low, posed and candid.
  • Sort as you go: keepers up, trash out, follow-ups flagged to a director the same day.

02 · Tonight, 10:00 to 11:15

Night sky photography

Open to anyone on staff, not just the media team. No experience needed. Bring whatever you shoot with, a phone included: phones do more of this than most people expect. Where we gather gets named at dinner.

Why this hour

The moon rises at 11:33 tonight, so the whole session sits in the dark part of the night. Partly cloudy, a slight chance of a passing shower before 11, fog building after midnight. Cloud does not cancel this. The settings are the same, and a sky with something in it often makes the better photograph.

Bring

  • Your camera or your phone, with a charged battery. Cold and long exposures both eat battery.
  • A tripod if camp has one. If not, anything that holds a camera still: a rail, a dock post, a rolled-up sweatshirt on a picnic table.
  • A layer. It gets cool on the water at that hour.
  • A headlamp or phone light, and mercy for everyone's night vision: point it down, and red light if you have it. Eyes take twenty minutes to adjust and one flash to lose it.

The settings we will use

Written here so you have them after tonight. On a camera, in manual (M):

  • Focus: manual, set on a star. Autofocus cannot find the dark. Switch the lens to MF, turn on live view, zoom the screen in on the brightest star you can find, and turn the ring until it is the smallest point it will make. Then do not touch it again. This is the step that decides whether the night is worth anything.
  • Aperture: as wide as your lens goes. The smallest f-number you have, f/2.8 or f/3.5 or f/4. You need every bit of the light.
  • Shutter: 15 to 20 seconds. Longer than that and the stars stop being points and start being short streaks, because the earth is turning. The wider your lens, the longer you can hold before that shows.
  • ISO: 1600 to 3200. Start at 1600 and raise it if the frame is too dark. Grain is fine. Grain is not the problem; blur is.
  • Two-second timer. Your finger on the shutter shakes the camera for the first second of a twenty-second exposure. Let the timer trip it.
  • White balance around 4000K if you can set it, or auto and we fix it later. Shoot RAW if your camera offers it.

On a phone: night mode, propped on something solid, and do not touch it while it counts down. Newer iPhones will run a ten to thirty second night exposure if the phone is perfectly still, and that is enough for the Milky Way on a clear night.

What we will actually try

The first frame that works is the one people remember, so we start with the sky over the water and get everyone one. After that: a person lit for a second by a headlamp against the stars, the lit buildings of camp with the sky behind them, and if the clouds cooperate, long enough on one frame to see the stars move.

04 · What we're after

Faces, moments, care

Faces first

Parents scan every photo for one thing: their kid. A gorgeous shot of the lake with nobody in it is a postcard. A clear, happy face is the product.

A variety of perspectives

Wide and tight. High and low. Horizontal and vertical. Posed and candid. The day's set should feel like a day at camp, not a contact sheet of the same shot.

The camera is camper care

Thirty seconds at the back of the camera, showing a kid their photo, tells them somebody sees them. Pull kids together for a shot and you have built a moment, not just recorded one. You are not a photographer visiting camp. You are camp, holding a camera.

Getting the pose, and the smile

Engage kids where they are, with what they are doing, during a pause in the program. While they are waiting their turn, for instance. Introduce yourself, and before one or more of the group heads off, ask to get a photo. You do this once with every kid and they will pose for you. They will mirror your expression. Shoot joyfully.

05 · Where the photos are

Working the windows

Every morning, look at the day's schedule and make a short list of what you will shoot and when. Pick things that are active, and pick spaces that are easy to photograph. Faces and energy from the activity, good light and room to move from the space. Plan around both.

The gathering, several times a day

Wherever the whole camp comes together before meals is the easiest place all week to get faces. Every face at camp, more than once a day. Shoot wide for the gathering, then go tight on small groups. Kids being silly, doing bits: catch it. More than one kid in frame beats a solo shot.

The waterfront, best light

Kids helping each other, hauling boats, out on the water. Shoot the pairs, the clusters, the small groups off on their own. Two kids working together to get a boat back in is a perfect frame every time.

Performances and evening programs

Take the stage-from-the-audience shot once. The better photos face the other way. Shoot the audience from the stage, zoom in on the happy faces, and anticipate the laugh: the reaction is coming, so be aimed at faces when it lands.

Shabbat, Friday evening, and Havdalah after dark Saturday

The two hardest and best frames of the weekend, and both are low light. No flash: it kills the thing you are photographing. Open the aperture as wide as it goes, raise the ISO until the shutter is back at 1/60, hold still, and take two of everything. A grainy sharp photo is a keeper; a clean blurry one is trash. We shoot these together.

06 · Making good frames

The frame, and the sharpness

Make the frame

  • Get close, then take three steps closer. Fill the frame with people.
  • Shoot at their eye level. Kneel for younger campers; looking down at kids flattens every photo.
  • Clean up the background. Step left or right to lose the trash can, the golf cart, the pile of life jackets.
  • Zoom with your feet before you zoom with the lens.

Make it sharp

  • Wipe the lens first. A smudged lens ruins more photos than anything else at camp.
  • Focus on the face: put the focus point on a kid's eye, half-press to lock, then shoot.
  • Put light on faces. Have subjects face the light, and never shoot with the sun or a bright window directly behind them.
  • Two hands on the camera. Hold the shutter for a burst when there is action.

Choosing your focus point

Left on its own, the camera picks a focus point for you, and it usually picks whatever is nearest: a shoulder, a railing, the kid in front of the kid you wanted. Take the choice back.

  • Set the center point and keep it there. On most bodies the center point is the most accurate one. Make it your home. We do this on your camera in the first session.
  • Then use the move: focus, hold, recompose. Put the center point on the face (the eye when you are close), half-press until it locks, KEEP holding the half-press, and swing the frame to the composition you want. Press through. Sharp face, good frame, every time.
  • Wide scenes forgive; tight and low-light frames do not. Wide open on a fast lens, focus is inches deep: it has to be the eye, and lean back to recompose rather than stepping, or you will walk out of your own focus.
  • Running kids: keep the point on them, hold a burst, take the sharp one. Volume beats perfection on action.

Why a photo is bright or dark

Three settings control the light. Move one and you trade against the others.

  • Aperture, the f-number. Low (f/1.8) means a big opening, lots of light, a blurred background. High (f/8) means a small opening, less light, more of the scene in focus.
  • Shutter speed. Fast (1/500) freezes a kid mid-jump but needs more light. Slow (1/60) gathers light but blurs motion. Below 1/60 handheld, even still subjects go soft.
  • ISO. Low (100) is clean and needs bright light. High (1600) shoots in the dark but adds grain. Raise it only as far as you need to keep the shutter fast enough.

The working rule: in daylight, low ISO, let aperture and shutter do the work. As the light drops, raise ISO first, then open the aperture, and keep the shutter at or above 1/60.

07 · The dial

Modes, and when to leave the one you are in

There is no beginner mode and no serious mode. There is the mode that gets the frame in front of you. Most of camp gets shot in one or two of these, and knowing when to move is worth more than knowing all of them.

P with your own ISO

The camera picks the aperture and the shutter together, you set the ISO. This is a real working mode, and it is fast: it keeps you shooting instead of dialing while the moment goes by. If this is where you live, three things make it much stronger.

  • ISO is your shutter-speed lever. This is the whole trick of P. When you raise the ISO, the camera answers with a faster shutter. Indoors and in shade, going from 400 to 1600 is what turns a blurry frame into a sharp one. You are not making the photo brighter; you are buying speed.
  • Watch the shutter number, not the picture. Half-press and read it. Below 1/60 handheld, even a still kid goes soft. Kids moving want 1/250. Anything you would call sports wants 1/500 or faster. If the number is too low, raise ISO until it climbs.
  • Program shift. While the meter is awake, turn the main dial and the camera slides through different aperture and shutter pairs at the same brightness. One way gives you a wider aperture and a blurrier background, the other gives you a faster shutter. You get most of what Av and Tv do without leaving P. Nikon bodies show it as P*; Canon just changes the numbers.

Where P runs out: it does not know what you are trying to do. In one light it may hand you f/8 at 1/125 when you wanted f/2 at 1/1000. When you find yourself fighting it, that is the signal to move one notch.

Av or A, aperture priority

You pick the aperture, the camera picks the shutter. Move here when the background matters: f/1.8 to f/2.8 for one or two faces and the background falls away, f/8 for a group or an activity where you want the whole scene readable. Portraits, golden hour, anything posed.

Tv or S, shutter priority

You pick the shutter, the camera picks the aperture. This is the sports mode. Set 1/500 for running and field games, 1/1000 for a ball leaving a foot or a kid off the ground. Set it and stop thinking about it; you have enough to track already.

M, manual

You set everything. Worth it in two places: light that does not change, like a stage or a lit indoor room, where one correct setting holds all night, and the night sky, where every automatic mode guesses wrong. Section 02 has those settings.

Auto ISO with a shutter floor

If your body offers it, this is the setup that keeps a whole camp day sharp with the least thinking. Put the camera in Av, set Auto ISO, then find the minimum shutter speed setting and set it to 1/250. Now the camera will raise ISO on its own rather than let the shutter fall below your floor, which is the mistake that costs the most photos at camp. You still choose the look with the aperture. If you prefer your ISO by hand, keep it by hand; the floor rule is the same either way, you are just the one enforcing it.

Exposure compensation, the button worth learning

The +/- button works in P, Av, and Tv, and it is the fastest fix on the camera. The meter aims for an average, so it gets fooled in predictable ways. Backlit kid, a bright lake or sky behind them, anything white: the camera underexposes the face, so dial +1 and the face comes back. A spotlit performer on a dark stage: the camera overexposes, so dial -1. Set it back to zero when the light changes, because it stays where you left it.

One line to carry: in P, ISO buys shutter speed. Move to Tv for sports, Av when the background matters, M when the light stops changing.

09 · After the shot

Sorting, and the daily set

  • You shoot.
  • You sort, the same day: keepers, trash, and flags for follow-up. A day's work left unsorted is a day's work nobody can use.
  • Usable means sharp, lit, and alive: a photo a parent could screenshot and text to grandma.
  • Flag anything an adult should see, a visible injury, sunburn, or bandage, to a director the same day. It does not go in the set.
  • Last night's evening program goes into the next day's set.

10 · Looking to 2027

What would make next summer easier

Four things, in the order I would spend on them. None of this is needed to do good work this summer, and all of it changes how much work the same effort produces next summer. It is written here so the team has it in one place when the conversation about next year comes up.

First: the archive is on one hard drive

This is the only item on the page that is urgent rather than useful. One drive is not a backup. Drives fail with no warning, and the thing that disappears is camp history: every summer that has been shot so far, gone in a click that nobody hears. The working standard is three copies, on two kinds of storage, with one of them somewhere else. Cloud storage is the simplest way to get that third copy off the property, and it also means an alum's photo request in February does not depend on somebody finding a drive in a drawer.

Second: a machine that can actually edit

A laptop or desktop with at least 32GB of memory, and more if the budget reaches. Editing is where a media team's time goes, and an underpowered machine taxes every single day of the summer: the catalog crawls, previews build while you wait, exports run past bedtime. On a strong machine the same day's work takes a fraction of the evening. This is the purchase that buys back the most hours.

Third: a 50mm f/1.8

Nikon or Canon to match the bodies. It is the least expensive lens either company makes and the largest single jump in what a camera can do: faces with the background falling away, and enough light to shoot the dining hall, evening programs, and Havdalah without flash, which is where kit lenses give up. If only one thing on this list gets bought, buy this one, and buy one for each photographer.

Fourth: the archive organized, and kept that way

Folder structure, naming, and keywords, written down as a convention the whole team follows, so a photo can be found by what is in it rather than by who remembers taking it. This team already works this way, which is further than most camps get. What is worth adding is closing each summer deliberately: the season's work culled, named, tagged, backed up, and shut, so next June starts on an archive rather than on a pile.

Together these are the difference between a media team that survives the summer and one that compounds year over year, because everything shot stays findable and usable long after the campers go home.
